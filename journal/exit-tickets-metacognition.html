Exit tickets that teach students to think about their thinking

April 2025 Classroom 3-min read

The situation

"Metacognition is the ability to think about one's own thinking processes, to plan, monitor and assess one's understanding and performance." This is how developmental psychologist John Flavell, a pioneer of metacognition research, described it. I wanted my students to build these skills in the middle of a design project.

What I tried

I introduced exit tickets focused on metacognitive reflection. Instead of only asking what students had learned, I posed deeper prompts:

"What strategies or techniques help me come up with good ideas?" "What was one point of confusion, and how might you address it?" "How did I monitor my progress?" "If I could give advice to my past self at the start of this project, what would I say?"

What happened

The goal was not just reflection but awareness of how students learn, plan, monitor and adjust. On the line-following robot project, one student wrote that when the robot would not turn on, they kept testing until they found the problem. Their advice to their past self: check power first and test everything early.

What I learned

Doing this once or twice will not create lasting change. To see real impact, metacognitive reflection has to become a regular habit, embedded in classroom culture.

When we nurture metacognitive thinking consistently, students take full ownership of their learning journey and become not just better learners but self-aware, adaptable thinkers.
