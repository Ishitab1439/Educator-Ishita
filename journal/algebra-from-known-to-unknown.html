From "monster" to manageable: introducing algebra

August 2024 Classroom 4-min read

The situation

One of my students once called algebra a "monster", a feeling many learners share. My Grade 7 class at AIMEE International School had just finished geometry and was about to start "Expressions, Formulae and Equations". I wanted to find out whether a lesson built on questions, rather than on explanation first, could help students who struggle with abstraction.

What I tried

I gave each group a few matchsticks and one challenge: form the letter L with two sticks, then add another L next to it, and make as many L's as you can. Then came a run of questions. What type of pattern is this? What made the pattern grow? How many matchsticks would 100 L's need, without using the matchsticks?

A worksheet table helped students record their counts. Only after they had spotted the pattern did I introduce the idea of a variable, as something whose value is not fixed. We repeated the idea with the letter F, and I closed by asking each student to invent an everyday problem and write a generalised statement for it.

What happened

Groups arrived at statements such as "the number of matchsticks is twice the number of L's." The next day, two groups had already worked out that a letter like n, k or t could stand for the number of L's. The other groups cross-questioned them ("How can we use alphabets in Maths?") and the class buzzed with arguments and negotiations. Students were no longer passengers; they were co-pilots in the lesson.

What I learned

Starting from the known and moving gradually to the unknown meant every student could take part, whatever their starting point. Well-chosen questions did the work that explanation usually does. I wrote the project up as a case study, "Igniting Inquiry: A Journey from Basic Questions to Deeper Understanding", which was published in the Birmingham City University Education Journal.
